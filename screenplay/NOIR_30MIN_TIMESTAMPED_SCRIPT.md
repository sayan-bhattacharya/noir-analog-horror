# NOIR — The Nishidak Broadcast
## Timestamped Master Screenplay

**Version:** Revised dramatic and presentation pass  
**Runtime:** 30:22  
**Picture:** 4:3 archival image within a 16:9 master; monochrome with limited amber, red, and tactile yellow accents  
**Sound:** *Noir.wav*, original score by Atandra (DroneWatch), full-length master  
**Form:** Silent visual narrative. All story information is carried by image, performance, sound, and a small number of diegetic broadcast cards.

> **Premise:** During an unlisted late-night emergency broadcast, a solitary clerk follows a familiar voice into the Bengal countryside. A black liquid, an impossible cat, and a watching eye draw him into a fall that ends in a corridor where the rules of the broadcast still apply.

---

## Dramatic approach

The film earns each escalation. It begins with a signal that could be ordinary tape damage, moves through a recognizably human night, and only gradually makes the landscape impossible. The corridor is allowed to feel calm before it becomes threatening. Hold on images long enough for the audience to notice the change; do not announce every anomaly with a new card, glitch, or cut.

### Character

**Bimal Babu — the witness.** A tired, practical clerk, accustomed to following instructions and keeping private worries to himself. His first response is to listen; his second is to verify. He goes outside because the voice sounds familiar, but he does not become a passive prop: he checks the pump, marks the path, and later tests the corridor's rule. His small choices give the uncanny events emotional weight. By the end, he understands that the corridor is repeating the broadcast's logic, even if he cannot escape it.

**The caller.** Never shown. Familiarity is the threat: a voice implied by the audience's own memory, not a monster given a face.

**The cat / hyper-object.** Deadpan and almost ridiculous at first glance. Its stillness makes the impossible extension more disturbing. It does not attack or chase Bimal; it marks a passage between scales.

**The staring man.** An ordinary figure whose unreadability echoes Bimal's own stillness. No overt villain performance. His connection to the broadcast and the eye remains suggestive, not explained.

### Image and pacing rules

- Begin with locked frames and slow, motivated movement. Increase instability only as Bimal loses his bearings.
- Keep the rural sections tactile and specific: damp plaster, cotton netting, red-oxide floor, rust, wet soil, bamboo, and wind.
- Let the cat's absurd silhouette register before its neck begins to extend. Avoid a rapid monster reveal.
- At the hinge, remove visual density as the score falls away. The first corridor image should feel almost relieving.
- Use archival grain and CRT artifacts as a frame language, not a constant effect layer. Reserve the strongest tearing and color separation for the rupture and ending.
- Keep the spectrogram and playhead discreet. It is an orientation motif at chapter transitions, not a permanent HUD competing with the story.

---

## On-screen text system

Text is part of the world. Each treatment has one job and one visual identity.

| Text class | Treatment | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Archive slate** | Centered, restrained typewriter or broadcast sans; warm white on black; generous margins | Date, station, and the opening warning |
| **Emergency broadcast** | Compact phosphor-green or amber OSD; fixed position; occasional tracking loss | Channel, timecode, jurisdiction, and official instructions |
| **Rule card** | One short sentence, white on near-black; no decorative frame | A rule Bimal encounters or chooses to test |
| **Environmental text** | Existing signage only; natural perspective and lighting | Corridor sign, platform markings, local labels |

### Copy and timing

- Prefer one readable thought per card. Avoid stacking Bengali and English translations simultaneously over action; use a clean translation card or a separate beat.
- Establish Bengali text in correct Unicode and have a Bengali proofreader review spelling, register, and line breaks before picture lock. English text below is the approved working copy where Bengali proofing is pending.
- Leave each card on screen long enough to read at normal speed. Do not animate every character; reserve a brief type-on for the first emergency notice only.
- Keep line length short and punctuation spare. Never use multiple exclamation marks, shouted all-caps paragraphs, or explanatory technical jargon as a substitute for suspense.
- Let text change only when the audience has time to notice it. A single altered word is more unsettling than a page of glitching copy.

## Timestamped screenplay

### PROLOGUE — THE SIGNAL (00:00–01:30)

**00:00–00:30 — Black glass**  
Black-and-white CRT raster, nearly lost in darkness. A thin circular mark sits at the center of the screen. It could be a flaw in the glass. A single black droplet hangs beneath it. Hold. The score enters as a low electrical presence; no title yet.

**00:30–01:00 — The drop**  
The droplet trembles, falls, and touches a faint ring of water. The ripple expands once, then disappears into black. A small red playhead begins its quiet progress along the spectrogram at the lower edge.

**01:00–01:30 — Archive slate**  
The image resolves into a dated broadcast slate. The station identification appears first; after a pause, the warning follows.

> **MAY 14, 1994**  
> **DO NOT ANSWER A FAMILIAR VOICE AFTER MIDNIGHT.**

The line holds. The final word loses sync for a fraction of a second. Cut to the night sky over a flat, empty field.

### ACT I — THE HOUSE AND THE CALL (01:30–06:00)

**01:30–02:30 — The solitary house**  
EXT. NISTOBDHOPUR — NIGHT. A two-storey ancestral house stands alone in waterlogged fields. Banana leaves move in the wind. The camera holds at a distance; the house is small in the frame, the darkness larger still. No visible power lines. No nearby lights.

At the edge of the image, one upper shutter sits open by a few inches. Do not call attention to it with a push-in.

**02:30–03:15 — A change in the leaves**  
The wind shifts. Most leaves bend with it. One broad leaf turns against it. Bimal appears in the upper window, still and listening. A faint light moves behind him, then goes out.

An emergency OSD fades in at the screen edge, as if already present on the recording:

> **VHF 04 / DD-1 BANGLA**  
> **SECTOR: NISTOBDHOPUR**

**03:15–04:15 — Inside the room**  
INT. BIMAL'S BEDROOM — NIGHT. Bimal sits on the edge of the bed in his rumpled work clothes. His shoes are aligned neatly beneath it. He listens toward the open window. A familiar call is suggested in the score's changing tone; no intelligible voice is heard.

He looks toward the doorway. Waits. Looks back to the window. He puts on his shoes.

**04:15–05:15 — The mosquito net**  
Bimal passes beneath the hanging mosquito net. Its hem brushes his shoulder and continues to sway after he has crossed. He notices, steadies the fabric with one hand, then lets go. The room is still.

For a moment, his shadow on the wall seems to have an extra joint. Bimal turns. The shadow is ordinary again.

**05:15–06:00 — The threshold**  
Bimal opens the front door. He does not step out immediately. He checks the courtyard, then the path to the handpump. A rule card appears only after he has crossed the threshold:

> **RULE 01**  
> **DO NOT FOLLOW A VOICE YOU CANNOT PLACE.**

Bimal looks back at the house. He continues toward the pump.

### ACT II — THE CONDUIT (06:00–10:00)

**06:00–07:15 — The handpump**  
EXT. COURTYARD — NIGHT. The rusted chapa-kol stands beside the banana grove. Its handle moves once, slowly, with no hand on it. Bimal stops several paces away. He looks behind the pump, then at the wet ground. Nothing else moves.

He tests the handle. It resists, then sinks by itself. A thick black liquid gathers at the spout. It catches a thin highlight; its surface is almost too reflective to read.

**07:15–08:00 — The pool**  
The liquid reaches the soil and spreads. It should soak in. Instead, it draws the red mud inward, forming a shallow, perfect circle. Bimal picks up a fallen twig and touches the edge. The twig vanishes below the surface without a splash.

He steps back. The camera stays with the pool.

**08:00–09:15 — False calm**  
The pump stops. The score thins. Bimal marks the pool's edge with a small stone and walks several steps away. The reflection in the black surface shows a patch of sky that is not visible above the camera. A slow ripple passes through that reflection alone.

Bimal returns to the stone. It now rests inside the pool, though the surrounding mud is undisturbed. He looks toward the house, weighing whether to go back.

**09:15–10:00 — The message**  
Cut to an empty verandah. A lantern moves gently in still air. Return to the pool. A single broadcast line appears, steady and legible:

> **THE WATER DOES NOT COME FROM BELOW.**

The line remains for a beat after the image cuts to black.

### ACT III — THE WITNESS IN THE SKY (10:00–14:00)

**10:00–11:30 — The figure on the fence**  
EXT. COURTYARD — NIGHT. A gaunt black cat sits on the split-bamboo fence. Its body is small against the field. Its silhouette is faintly geometric, but still reads as an animal at rest. Bimal sees it and stops. The cat does not look at him.

The camera holds long enough for the eye to find the cat without a cut or musical sting.

**11:30–12:30 — The impossible measure**  
The cat's neck lengthens a little. It pauses. Bimal glances toward the sky, then back to the fence, as if checking his own perception. The neck extends again, passing through the cloud line. The cat's body remains perfectly still.

The score grows denser, but the image does not speed up. The cat's head disappears into the cloud cover.

**12:30–13:30 — The third eye**  
The cloud deck opens around a single pale shape. It resolves gradually into an enormous eye. Its iris is the only red element in the image. It does not search. It looks down the line of the cat's impossible neck, then settles on Bimal.

Bimal takes one step backward. The eye does not react.

**13:30–14:00 — Recognition**  
The eye's pupil contracts to a small point. For one frame, its circular outline matches the mark from the prologue. No archival clip is needed; let the visual rhyme carry the transition. The image shudders once and steadies.

### ACT IV — THE FALL (14:00–18:00)

**14:00–15:30 — The ground gives way**  
The black pool has widened beneath Bimal. A hairline crack reaches his shoe. He looks down and moves to step away. The soil tilts—not violently, but with the quiet certainty of a surface becoming vertical. He reaches for the fence. His fingers close on empty air.

Bimal falls backward. The camera remains with him as the courtyard slips upward and out of frame. The black pool becomes a circular opening, not a splashing liquid surface.

**15:30–16:30 — Descent**  
Bimal falls through a dark space marked by thin horizontal scanlines. The cat's stretched neck passes at a distance like a transmission mast glimpsed from a moving train. He turns once, trying to orient himself. There is no stable up or down.

The spectrogram playhead crosses the score's natural drop. Reduce image detail as the sound recedes; do not add impacts to manufacture momentum.

**16:30–18:00 — White**  
Bimal grows smaller against the void. A faint fluorescent hum begins before the image changes. The fall slows. His outline becomes a pale point, then disappears into a soft white field. Hold on the white for several seconds. Cut cleanly to the corridor.

### ACT V — THE CORRIDOR (18:00–30:22)

**18:00–19:30 — Awakening**  
INT. UNDERGROUND CORRIDOR — UNKNOWN. Bimal stands beneath fluorescent lights in a spotless tiled passage. The yellow tactile strip is the only surviving color. He checks his hands, his shoes, his sleeves. No mud. No injury. The sound is almost empty.

He looks behind him. The corridor is clear. He walks forward, slowly, keeping one hand against the wall.

**19:30–21:00 — A rule for the corridor**  
At the end of the passage, a sign reads **EXIT 8**. Bimal approaches it. The lights flicker once. He turns back to check the route he has taken; the same corridor continues behind him.

A simple white-on-black card appears between shots, not over Bimal:

> **IF YOU NOTICE A CHANGE, TURN BACK.**  
> **IF NOTHING HAS CHANGED, KEEP WALKING.**

Bimal studies the words. He turns around and walks back the way he came.

**21:00–23:00 — The man**  
At the next bend, a pale middle-aged man in a dark grey suit stands on the yellow strip, holding a briefcase. He is distant and still. Bimal stops. The two men look at one another.

The camera holds on Bimal's face. He glances at the nearest ceiling light, waits for its next flicker, then looks back. The suited man appears slightly closer. His feet remain planted.

**23:00–25:00 — The test**  
Bimal takes one step toward the man, then another. The distance between them changes only when the fluorescent lights blink. Bimal understands this pattern and tries to use it: he closes his eyes before the next flicker, then opens them. The man is closer again.

Bimal turns and walks toward the EXIT 8 sign. He passes the same wall mark twice. On the second pass, a thin line of black liquid seeps from the grout. He stops, touches the wall, and rubs the liquid between finger and thumb. It leaves no stain.

**25:00–28:00 — The broadcast returns**  
The corridor remains white and quiet. The black seam lengthens by a few inches. The EXIT 8 sign flickers. For a single beat, the numeral is replaced by **VHF 04**. It returns to normal before Bimal can be sure he saw it.

He looks toward the camera for the first time. Not pleading. Not resigned. He has recognized the grammar of the broadcast: a rule, a warning, a corridor that repeats. He starts walking again, deliberately counting the lights as he passes beneath them.

**28:00–29:40 — The mirror**  
Bimal reaches the suited man. The briefcase is empty when the man opens it. Inside, a small black CRT screen shows the courtyard from the opening act. Bimal sees the cat on the fence. He looks up at the man's face.

In the man's eye, the Doordarshan spiral appears as a dim reflection. The cat stands behind Bimal in the corridor, but when Bimal turns, there is only the yellow strip and the receding lights.

The suited man closes the briefcase. No explanation. No sudden movement.

**29:40–30:22 — Terminal lock**  
Return to the spectrogram. The red playhead reaches the end. The score resolves toward near-silence. Cut to black. After a short pause, the thin circular mark from the opening appears alone. No droplet this time.

Hold for four seconds. End.

## Production notes

- The time ranges are editorial guideposts; preserve the listed total runtime and let the score determine exact shot lengths within each passage.
- Treat the listed on-screen copy as final English working copy. Bengali versions require native-language proofing before rendering.
- Avoid literal instructions that encourage unsafe behavior in a real location; all actions are fictional and staged.
- Use source footage only after rights and license review. The screenplay no longer depends on recognizable excerpts from other films; original inserts can carry the same memory and disorientation beats.
- Keep the corridor's visual language distinct from the rural material. The contrast is the point; do not carry CRT distortion continuously across the transition.
